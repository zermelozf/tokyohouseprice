Formalizing the Decision Making

As mentioned in the previous section, the two properties are different, hence not strictly comparable. However, I will assume they both are "good enough", and satisfy whatever criteria the buyer/renter cares about.

With that said, we can focus on the financial aspect of the decision. And to that end, I will use two very powerful tools:

  • Net Present Value (NPV)
  • Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

But before we dive into the details, let's first formalize what we mean by a property.

Modeling a Property

A property is a bundle of cash flows.

In case we rent the property, the cashflow is pretty simple: we just pay an annual rent.

\[ CF_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(t) = -R \quad \text{{ '{' }}for {{ '}' }} t = 1, 2, \ldots, n \]

where \( R \) is the annual rent and \( n \) is the number of years.

If we buy the property, there are two types of cashflows:

  • Negative: down payment, mortgage, property taxes, maintenance, insurance, etc.
  • Positive: the proceeds from selling the property.

    • \[ CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(0) = -DP \]

    \[ CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(t) = -M - T - I - Ma \quad \text{{ '{' }}for {{ '}' }} t = 1, 2, \ldots, n-1 \]

    \[ CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) = S - M - T - I - Ma \]

    where \( DP \) is the down payment, \( M \) is the annual mortgage payment, \( T \) is property taxes, \( I \) is insurance, \( Ma \) is maintenance, \( S \) is the sale proceeds, and \(n\) the time of the sale.

    Let's take a visual look at the cashflows for the properties described above making the assumption that we will sell the property after 25 years.

    Now these cashflows will continue for as long as we rent, or until we sell the property. So the total value years is the sum of all the cashflows.

    \[ V_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) = \sum_{{ '{' }}t=0{{ '}' }}^{{ '{' }}n{{ '}' }} CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(t) \]

    \[ V_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(n) = \sum_{{ '{' }}t=0{{ '}' }}^{{ '{' }}n{{ '}' }} CF_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(t) \]

    For example, if we compare the 42万円 rent with the 99.7万円 buy options introduced in the previous section, we see that the rent and buy cumulative cashflow value cross on the {{ breakevenYear }}th year:

    Or in other words, it only makes sense to buy the property if we intend to live in it for more than {{ breakevenYear }} years (after which \( V_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) > V_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(n) \)). And the longer we live in the house, the more advantageous it is to buy.

    To make the comparison even easier, we can plot the difference between the two cumulative cashflows \(V_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) - V_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(n) \). Whenever, it is positive, it means that buying is better than renting.

    Opportunity Cost and Net Present Value (NPV)

    However, there is another factor to consider: the Opportunity Cost of buying vs renting.

    For example, let's say that instead of making a down payment for the house, we instead rent and invest the down payment in the stock market or in a remunerated account. Over time, that investment will grow at a some (perhaps unknown) rate \(r\).

    Similarly, if the mortgage we pay is less than the rent we would have paid, then we could invest the difference in the stock market. So in essence, we try to maximize the return of whatever money we have left over in either case.

    If we follow that approach, then the future value of the cashflow difference will be:

    \[ FV_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) = \sum_{{ '{' }}t=0{{ '}' }}^{{ '{' }}n{{ '}' }} (CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(t) - CF_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(t)) \times (1 + r)^{{ '{' }}n-t{{ '}' }} \quad (1) \]

    \( FV_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n)\) is called the Future Value of buying because it corresponds to the net financial position a buyer will have compared to a renter (at year \(n\)).

    But \(FV\) will only be available in the future. So, how much is that amount worth today?

    One way to answer that question is to imaging that someone gave us a one time lump sum today. Then we invest it in the same account or stock market with return \(r\). What sum would give us the same \(FV_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n)\) in the future? That one time lump sum is

    \[ PV_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) = \frac{{ '{' }}FV_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n){{ '}' }}{{ '{' }}(1 + r)^n{{ '}' }} \quad (2) \]

    This is called the Present Value (PV) of the buy option. We can also express this directly in terms of cash flows:

    \[ PV_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) = \sum_{{ '{' }}t=0{{ '}' }}^{{ '{' }}n{{ '}' }} \frac{{ '{' }}CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(t) - CF_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(t){{ '}' }}{{ '{' }}(1 + r)^t{{ '}' }} \quad (3) \]

    Present Value (PV) is a very powerful tool as it allows us to immediately compare options in terms of current wealth. In a way, the simple act of deciding to buy or rent (and sell after n years) will immediately increase (or decrease) our wealth by \( PV_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) \).

    Qualitative Analysis: Understanding the Mechanics

    Before diving into numbers, let's understand the fundamental mechanics of the rent vs buy decision.

    Buying as Saving Rent

    When you buy a house, you're essentially "saving" the rent you would otherwise pay. However, this saving comes with additional costs and responsibilities that renters don't face.

    Additional Costs of Ownership

    • Property taxes (typically 1-2% of property value annually)
    • Maintenance and repairs (2-3% of property value annually)
    • Insurance premiums
    • Transaction costs (agent fees, legal fees)

    The Leverage Factor

    Most buyers use mortgage financing, which introduces leverage. This amplifies both gains and losses: if property values rise, your returns are magnified, but if they fall, your losses are also amplified. The loan also creates a fixed obligation regardless of market conditions.

    Edge Cases and Long-term Considerations

    Several scenarios can significantly impact the rent vs buy decision:

    • Short holding periods: Transaction costs can outweigh benefits if you move within 3-5 years
    • Market downturns: Leverage can lead to negative equity situations
    • Interest rate changes: Rising rates increase borrowing costs and can affect property values
    • Maintenance surprises: Major repairs can significantly impact total cost of ownership

    Quantitative Analysis: NPV of Cash Flow Differences

    The quantitative analysis boils down to comparing the Net Present Value of cash flow differences between buying and renting.

    Cash Flow Difference Calculation

    For each year, we calculate: (Cost of Buying) - (Cost of Renting). A positive NPV indicates that buying is financially advantageous.

    Annual Cash Flow Difference

    CF_t = (Rent_t + Rent_increases) - (Mortgage_payment + Property_tax + Maintenance + Insurance)

    Plus end-of-period considerations like property appreciation and down payment opportunity cost.

    Key Assumptions in Our Model

    Property Depreciation

    Buildings depreciate over time while land typically appreciates. Our model uses straight-line depreciation for the building component over the specified amortization period.

    Opportunity Cost

    The return you could earn by investing your down payment in alternative investments (typically 6-8% annually for stock market returns).

    Transaction Costs

    Include real estate agent fees, loan origination costs, and other one-time expenses associated with purchasing property.

    Maintenance and Operating Costs

    Annual maintenance costs calculated as a percentage of property value, including repairs, renovations, property taxes, and insurance.

    Sensitivity Analysis Using Monte Carlo

    Real estate markets are inherently uncertain. Our calculator uses Monte Carlo-style sensitivity analysis to help you understand how different scenarios might affect your decision.

    Monte Carlo Approach

    By adjusting key parameters and observing how they affect the NPV and IRR, you can stress-test your decision against various market conditions and personal circumstances.

    Key Factors to Test

    • Property appreciation rates (can be negative in declining markets)
    • Rent inflation rates and renewal costs
    • Interest rate changes and loan terms
    • Maintenance cost variations
    • Different holding periods (how long you stay)
    • Economic downturns and market volatility

    Sensitivity Insight

    If your decision remains consistent across a wide range of scenarios, you can be more confident in your choice. If small changes in assumptions flip your decision, you may want to gather more data or consider the risks more carefully.

    Conclusion

    The rent-or-buy decision is fundamentally about optimizing your financial future while maintaining your desired quality of life. By using NPV and IRR analysis, combined with sensitivity testing, you can make an informed decision based on your specific situation.

    Remember that financial models are tools to inform your decision, not make it for you. Consider your personal circumstances, risk tolerance, career stability, and long-term goals. The "right" decision varies for each person and depends on both financial and non-financial factors.

    Our calculator provides the analytical framework, but the final decision remains yours. Use these tools to understand the trade-offs, but don't forget to consider the intangible benefits of homeownership or the flexibility that renting provides.
