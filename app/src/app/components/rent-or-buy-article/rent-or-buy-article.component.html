A concrete example from SUUMO.jp

When it comes to housing, you usually have two options: renting or buying. This whole article is about making this decision as informed as possible. And to make things a little bit more concrete, I pulled a couple of properties from SUUMO.jp that we'll use for calculations throughout the article.

Example Properties

For Sale
  • Location: Shimomeguro 4, Meguro-ku, Tokyo
  • Layout: 3LDK
  • Number of floors: 2
  • Floor Area: 78.53m²
  • Age: 21 years
  • Price: ¥99,700,000
  • Station: 16min walk to JR Meguro
For Rent
  • Location: Meguro 4, Meguro-ku, Tokyo
  • Layout: 3LDK
  • Number of floors: 3
  • Floor Area: 99.4m²
  • Age: 15 years
  • Rent: ¥420,000/month
  • Station: 15min walk to JR Meguro

Let's be clear: the two properties are not perfectly comparable. While they are located in the same neighborhood, close to Meguro Station, the renting option is much bigger and has more floors.

However, I'll assume they both are reasonable alternatives for, say, a young couple with two kids. And thus, in the rest of the article, I'll focus on comparing them from a financial standpoint.

The Decision Making Framework

There are many factors at play when choosing the perfect house that suits your needs. From a financial perspective however, we can compare any two options with the tools below:

  • Net Present Value (NPV)
  • Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

But before we dive into the details, let's first formalize what we mean by a property.

Modeling a Property

A property is a bundle of cash flows.

In case we rent the property, the cash flow is pretty simple: we just pay a monthly rent (though for simplicity, we'll do the accounting yearly below).

\[ CF_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(t) = -R \quad \text{{ '{' }}for {{ '}' }} t = 1, 2, \ldots, n \]

where \( R \) is the annual rent and \( n \) is the number of years.

In case we buy the property though, there are two types of cash flows:

  • Negative: down payment, mortgage, property taxes, maintenance, insurance, etc.
  • Positive: the proceeds from selling the property.

\[ CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(0) = -DP - F \]

\[ CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(t) = -M - C \quad \text{{ '{' }}for {{ '}' }} t = 1, 2, \ldots, n-1 \]

\[ CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) = S - M - C \]

where \( DP \) is the down payment required to buy the property, \( F \) is the transaction costs or fees paid to a broker, \( M \) is the annual mortgage payment, \( C \) is the annual costs (property taxes, maintenance, insurance, etc.), \( S \) is the sale proceeds when the property is finally sold.

For example, let's compare the cash flows for the two housing options described above. I'll use {{ fees / 2 | percent }} for the broker fees (both when buying and selling) and a {{ loanYears }} years mortgage with a {{ mortgageRate | percent }} interest rate. Let's also assume running costs (maintenance, taxes, etc.) are {{ costs | percent }} of the property value.

Here's what our cash flow chart looks like if we sell the property after {{ simulationYears }} years:

Now, these cash flows represent how much money we spend or gain each year. If we want to see how much we spent overall, we can just sum them all together to see how "expensive" each option is.

\[ V_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) = \sum_{{ '{' }}t=0{{ '}' }}^{{ '{' }}n{{ '}' }} CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(t) \]

\[ V_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(n) = \sum_{{ '{' }}t=0{{ '}' }}^{{ '{' }}n{{ '}' }} CF_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(t) \]

For example, if we compare the ¥420,000 monthly rent with the ¥99,700,000 buy with a mortgage option, we see that the total costs cross in the {{ breakevenYear }}th year:

Or in other words, it only makes sense to buy the property if we intend to live in it for more than {{ breakevenYear }} years. Also, the longer we live in the house, the more advantageous it is to buy.

Mathematically, buying becomes advantageous when \( V_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) > V_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(n) \).

To better understand why we need {{ breakevenYear }} years to break even, let's consider the two aspects of paying back the loan:

  • ① Part of the monthly payment are forced savings we make instead of paying a rent.
  • ② The other part is used to pay the bank interest.

Only ② is a real cost as everything we invest in the house is money we will get back when we sell it.


Now, the question is: how long does it take to get the broker fees back thansk to the savings in ①?


A quick back of the enveloppe calculation tells us that with brokerage fees of {{ fees | percent }} and a forced rent saving of {{ rentYield | percent: '1.1-1' }}, minus {{ costs | percent }} in runnning costs and {{ mortgageRate | percent }} in interests, we are saving about {{ rentYield - costs - mortgageRate | percent }} every year. Thus it should take about {{ fees | percent }} / {{ rentYield - costs - mortgageRate | percent }} = {{ fees / (rentYield - costs - mortgageRate) | number: '1.0-0' }} years to get the broker fees back.


In reality, it takes a bit less because we are only paying {{ mortgageRate | percent }} interests on the remaining loan balance and not the total property price.

We can also plot the difference between the two cumulative cash flows to make it even easier to visualize. This will be useful when comparing with the NPV analysis in the next section.

Specifically, we plot \(V_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) - V_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(n) \).

Another important thing to note is that the slope of the curve is equal to the forced savings {{ rentYield | percent }} minus the {{ costs | percent }} running costs and the interest. So it seems we have an incentive to reduce our loan to maximize how fast we can break even. However, we will see in the next section that this is not necessarily the best strategy.

Opportunity Cost and Net Present Value (NPV)

There is another factor to consider: the Opportunity Cost of buying vs renting.

For example, let's say that instead of making a big down payment and a small loan for the house, we instead rent and invest the down payment in the stock market or in a remunerated account. Over time, that investment will grow at some rate \(r\).

Similarly, if the mortgage we pay is less than the rent we would have paid, then we could invest the difference in the stock market.

Building on this idea, we can try to maximize the Future Value (FV) of our bank account by investing whatever money we have left over each year.

\[ FV_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) = \sum_{{ '{' }}t=0{{ '}' }}^{{ '{' }}n{{ '}' }} (CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(t) - CF_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(t)) \times (1 + r)^{{ '{' }}n-t{{ '}' }} \quad (1) \]

where \(r\) is the opportunity cost rate.

But this future value will only be available in the future. So, how much is that amount worth today?

Even better, we can convert that Future Value into a Present Value (PV)! This is done by imagining that we receive a lump sum today (not incrementally and in the future) and invest it at a rate \(r\) for the same number of years \(n\).

\[ PV_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) = \frac{{ '{' }}FV_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n){{ '}' }}{{ '{' }}(1 + r)^n{{ '}' }} \quad (2) \]

We can also express this directly in terms of cash flows:

\[ PV_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(n) = \sum_{{ '{' }}t=0{{ '}' }}^{{ '{' }}n{{ '}' }} \frac{{ '{' }}CF_{{ '{' }}buy{{ '}' }}(t) - CF_{{ '{' }}rent{{ '}' }}(t){{ '}' }}{{ '{' }}(1 + r)^t{{ '}' }} \quad (3) \]

Let's again compare the two options, but using the Present Value of buying a house with a {{ opportunityCost | percent: '1.1-1' }} opportunity cost this time.

The buy vs. rent recommendation completely changes!


It now takes 9 years instead of 4 to break even, and the renting option looks much more attractive in the long run. In fact, buying is only a good option during a specific 8-year window.

I personally find this result quite interesting! Before doing the analysis, I would have never thought that timing the sale of your house actually mattered!

Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

OK, so using NPV to assess the buy vs rent decision, we get a better sense of whether we should buy, and also when we should sell. However, this tells a story in terms of absolute amounts of money, and if we really want to optimize, we could sell one house at the right timing and immediately buy another one and so on so as to maximize the slope of the NPV profile.

For that reason, it makes sense to assess the rate of return of the investment for each year. We know that depending on the timing, the rate of return of our investment will be more or less than {{ opportunityCost | percent: '1.1-1' }} thanks to the NPV profile. However, we don't know exactly the value of our rate of return for each year.

This is what the Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is for. There isn't a simple closed formula for the IRR, but we can use a numerical method to compute it from the undiscounted cash flows.

Again a surprising result. To be optimal, we should sell at year 11 according to the IRR calculation. This is one year earlier that what the NPV identifies as the optimal year to maximize the value of just one house investment.

In that graph, we can see the leverage effect of borrowing!


If we buy and sell the property immediately, we will lose the broker fees and get nothing. So the IRR is negative. But as we hold on to the property, the IRR will increase.


In the longer run, the rate of return is the rent we save ({{ rentYield | percent: '1.1-1' }} when expressed as a percentage of the buy price), minus the cost ratio of {{ costs | percent: '1.1-1' }}, which equals {{ rentYield - costs | percent: '1.1-1' }}. That is the return the house investment tends to the longer we hold it for a very long time.


However, since we borrow money, in the beginning, we will only have invested a small fraction of the total price of the house. But we will still get {{ rentYield - costs | percent: '1.1-1' }} of the total price of the house. This is why the IRR is peaking higher than just the rent yield.

Digression: Mortgages and Leveraged Buyouts

IRR is useful to assess pretty much any type of investment. In fact, some time ago I remember discussing the concept of Leveraged Buyouts with a CEO whose company was acquired by a Japanese Venture Capital firm.

A Leveraged Buyout is a type of acquisition where a company is bought using a significant amount of debt and thus, resembles the acquisition of a house through a mortgage. Often times, there is pressure on the investor to improve the financials of the company and sell it for a profit within a few years.

At the time it sounded counter-intuitive to me that the investor would want to sell the company rapidly. If the restructuring worked and the company prospects improved, then wouldn't it be a great long-term investment?

That may be so. However, leverage is also a powerful force that can greatly increase the return on investment of someone who is willing to take on the associated risk (like a Venture Capitalist looking for above market returns).

In fact, all things being equal, and even without improving the profitability of the company, it might still make sense to buy it with debt just to be able to multiply the natural return of the company using leverage. And you could imagine a group of VC firms buying and selling companies to each other with the right timing just to maximize their IRR.

Of course, this is a gross oversimplification. And in the case of a Leveraged Buyout as well as a house mortgage, the risk factor plays a big part in the decision.

Conclusion and Improvements

The rent-or-buy decision is fundamentally about optimizing your financial future while maintaining your desired quality of life. By using NPV and IRR analysis, I believe one can make a more informed decision.
